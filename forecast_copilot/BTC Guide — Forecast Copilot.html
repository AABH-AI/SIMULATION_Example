How Bend-The-Curve Works
A plain-language reference for the BTC adjustment concept and the parameters that drive it — not a full process manual, just the essentials.
What BTC Does

A statistical forecast only knows what history looks like — it has no way to see planned quality improvements, process changes, or organizational commitments that haven't happened yet. Bend-The-Curve (BTC) is the adjustment layer that reconciles the two: it takes the statistical forecast and nudges it toward a committed target, so the plan you publish reflects both what the data says and what the organization intends to make happen.

It's applied as a deliberate, incremental adjustment — not a hard override — so the forecast still looks like a plausible curve rather than a discontinuous jump.

Key Parameters
Modifier %
The primary lever. 100% means no change — the adjusted forecast equals the raw statistical forecast. Lower percentages pull the forecast down toward the target; e.g. an 80% modifier closes roughly 20% of the gap between forecast and target.
Ramp-In (Incremental Weeks)
The adjustment isn't applied as a single jump — it's spread and incremented gradually across the remaining forecast horizon, so the change accumulates over time instead of landing all at once in the first week.
Allocation Method
Once an adjustment is sized at a summary level, it's allocated back down to the lowest reporting dimensions using a weighted split that respects each dimension's existing share of volume — not an equal split across dimensions.
Segment Intersections
When a product or metric is tracked under more than one organizational hierarchy at once (e.g. two different business units, or multiple service types), the same modifier has to be entered separately for each intersection — it produces a different absolute number in each one, since each intersection starts from a different base volume.
Quarterly Phasing
A second, simpler adjustment layer applied after the annual-level sizing: a straight percentage scale-up or scale-down to hit each quarter's target, specifically to avoid an unnatural "flat cut every quarter" or "cliff-drop-then-spike" shape across the year.
New vs. Renewal Split
On the installed-base side, the same modifier concept is applied separately to new additions and to the renewal / retention rate — they respond to different business levers and are sized independently.

How This Suite Automates It

The rest of Forecast Copilot turns the manual sizing decision above into a data-driven one:

AI BTC Advisor
Offers three automated strategies in place of manually picking a modifier — Historical Best Fit (a statistical trend projection), Closest to AOP (sized directly off the gap to target), and Balanced (a blend of the two) — each with a confidence score.
BTC Distribution
Automates the allocation step: once a BTC % is selected, it's distributed across regions, product lines, and service types using a weighted split, and phased across weeks — the same allocation and phasing concepts above, computed instead of hand-built.

Why Automating This Matters
  • A manual, spreadsheet-driven version of this process usually means adjustments are entered as flat values rather than reusable formulas.
  • Zero-value periods and edge-case weeks often need special-case workarounds.
  • Visibility into an adjustment is typically limited until it flows all the way downstream into shared reporting.
  • Sizing a single change across every segment intersection by hand is slow and easy to get slightly out of sync.
  • An end-to-end pass across every input (forecast, service requests, installed base) commonly takes a few days per cycle when done by hand.
